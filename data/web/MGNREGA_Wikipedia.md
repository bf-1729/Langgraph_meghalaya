## Overview

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005 (MGNREGA) is an
Indian social welfare initiative designed to guarantee the "right to work".
Enacted on 23 August 2005 and implemented in February 2006, it is one of the
world's most expansive employment guarantee schemes. The legislation provides at
least 100 days of assured wage employment annually to rural household members who
volunteer for unskilled manual labour.

## Legislative Background

### Historical Context

Rural employment schemes in India evolved over decades: the Rural Manpower
Programme (from 1960), Crash Scheme for Rural Employment, Food for Work
Programme, National Rural Employment Programme, and Rural Landless Employment
Guarantee Programme. In April 1989 these were consolidated into the Jawahar
Rozgar Yojana, decentralising implementation through Panchayati Raj
Institutions. The Employment Assurance Scheme followed in October 1993,
targeting agricultural labourers during slack seasons. In 2001 several schemes
merged into the Sampoorna Grameen Rozgar Yojana (SGRY).

### Enactment and Implementation

An employment guarantee act was first proposed in 1991 by Prime Minister P. V.
Narasimha Rao. Minister of Rural Development Raghuvansh Prasad Singh introduced
the National Rural Employment Guarantee Bill on 22 March 2005. The Lok Sabha
passed it on 23 August 2005, the Rajya Sabha on 25 August 2005, and it received
presidential assent on 5 September 2005. Implementation commenced on 2 February
2006 under Prime Minister Manmohan Singh's United Progressive Alliance
government, initially in a pilot set of districts (about 200 of the most
backward districts, later reported as up to 625 districts in the pilot phase).
Coverage expanded nationwide from 1 April 2008 to all rural districts. The Act
was renamed after Mahatma Gandhi in 2009.

## Core Provisions

### Employment Guarantee Framework

The Act guarantees at least 100 days of wage employment per financial year to
one adult member of every rural household whose members volunteer for unskilled
manual work. The entitlement can be shared among household members. Work must be
provided within 15 days of application; failure triggers an unemployment
allowance — making employment a legal entitlement rather than discretionary
assistance.

### Permissible Works

Water conservation and water harvesting, drought-proofing and afforestation,
irrigation infrastructure, restoration of traditional water bodies, land
development, flood control and protection, rural connectivity, and other works
notified by the government.

### Wage Structure

Minimum wages with gender parity. Wages may be time-rated or piece-rated, with
states setting measurement norms and schedules of rates. Of project cost, 60%
must fund wages and 40% materials. Wages are indexed to the Consumer Price
Index — Agricultural Labour. As of 2022, state wage rates ranged from roughly
₹204 per day to ₹331 per day. Wage-payment delays (sometimes reported as long
as 200 days) have been a persistent problem; by 2021 about 99.7% of payments
were electronic (Direct Benefit Transfer via the National electronic Fund
Management System). Actual days of work provided have averaged around 45 per
household rather than the full 100.

### Women's Participation

The law reserves at least one-third of works for women; actual participation has
consistently been higher (around 47–50% nationally, and over 80% in some
states).

## Constitutional Foundation

MGNREGA operationalises Article 41 (right to work), draws on Article 48A
(environmental protection), supports Article 21 (livelihood and dignity), and
Article 16 (non-discrimination in employment). Articles 40 and 46 relate to
empowering village panchayats and the economic uplift of Scheduled Castes and
Scheduled Tribes. Gram Panchayats are the principal implementing authority,
reinforcing the 73rd Constitutional Amendment.

## Implementation Structure

### Operational Framework

Gram Panchayats hold primary implementation responsibility. A household
registers, receives a job card, applies for work, and is allocated employment
within the statutory timeframe. Single bank accounts for NREGA works, monthly
account squaring, and detailed registers (employment register, job cards, asset
register, muster rolls, complaint register) are maintained at village, block and
state levels.

### Technical Support

Technical Resource Support Groups are recommended at district, state and central
levels. A Monitoring and Information System (MIS) and the public NREGA website
support quality assurance.

### Social Audits

Social audits are mandatory. Public hearings (Jan Sunwai) at panchayat and
mandal levels examine official records obtained under the Right to Information
Act. The statute identifies eleven stages subject to continuous social-audit
scrutiny, from family registration and job-card distribution through work
selection, execution, wage payment, unemployment-allowance disbursement and the
mandatory social-audit forum.

## Performance and Outcomes

Independent studies found MGNREGA disproportionately reached Scheduled Castes,
Scheduled Tribes and illiterate households, raised agricultural wages
(especially for women), and reduced wage volatility (an insurance effect).
Research from the Indian Institute of Science projected that MGNREGA natural
resource management works could sequester significant carbon by 2030, a point
India cited to the UNFCCC. "MGNREGA Sameeksha", an anthology of studies compiled
with UNDP, was released on 14 July 2012.

The Comptroller and Auditor General's second performance audit (April 2007 –
March 2012), covering 3,848 gram panchayats across 28 states and 4 union
territories, found serious implementation lapses: declining employment, low work
completion (about 30%), weak planning in a third of panchayats, poor awareness
and staff shortages, MIS unreliability, and some states not having notified the
scheme.

## Asset Creation

Assets include rural roads and brick soling, land development and plantations,
livestock and poultry shelters, village tanks, ponds, check dams and irrigation
channels, and sanitation infrastructure. Over the past decade more than half of
NREGA funds have gone to water-related works; annual spend on rural water
bodies, wells and aquifers has exceeded ₹20,000 crore. Asset permanence and
maintenance remain debated.

## Convergence Initiatives

MGNREGA assets align with housing and sanitation schemes. The MPLADS scheme was
converged in 2012. Convergence needs inter-departmental cooperation but raises
concerns about diluting the income-security purpose.

## Criticism and Controversy

- **Corruption:** middlemen siphoning payments; ghost workers and inflated
  material rates. Electronic wage transfer and community monitoring are cited as
  mitigations.
- **Economic-efficiency critiques:** economists Jagdish Bhagwati and Arvind
  Panagariya called it "an inefficient instrument of shifting income to the
  poor"; Surjit Bhalla alleged high leakage.
- **Agricultural labour market:** critics argued it raised farm wages and
  reduced farm labour supply; contrary evidence noted farm work often paid more
  once meals and other benefits were counted.
- **Relevance debate:** a large fall in demand for MGNREGA work between 2020 and
  2025 was cited as evidence of reduced relevance; others attribute it to base
  effects from the COVID-era spike.

## Proposed Amendments and Policy Evolution

In 2014, a proposal to limit MGNREGA to backward blocks and change the
wage-material ratio from 60:40 to 51:49 drew wide criticism and was not adopted;
the government affirmed nationwide continuation. In September 2014 unions
launched a "Save MGNREGA" campaign (200 workdays, ₹300 daily wage, stricter
15-day guarantee). The 2017 Union Budget allocated ₹48,000 crore. From July 2019
MGNREGA became a key partner in the Jal Shakti Abhiyan water-conservation drive.

## Legacy and Assessment

MGNREGA is frequently described as the largest public-works and social-security
programme in the world. The World Bank, critical of employment restrictions in
2009, called it a "stellar example of rural development" in its 2014 World
Development Report. The scheme built rural infrastructure, raised rural wages,
gave livelihood security to millions, and expanded women's paid work, while
attracting ongoing debate over corruption and cost-effectiveness.
